The five levels of HAZWOPER emergency responders

From awareness to incident commander: what each level does, what it may not do and the minimum training.

29 CFR 1910.120(q)(6) sorts emergency responders by what they're expected to do, not by job title. Assign people to a level based on their role in your emergency response plan, then train them for it.

The levels

LevelRoleMinimum training
First responder, awarenessDiscovers the release, notifies and takes no further actionEnough training or demonstrated competency to do that role
First responder, operationsProtects people, property and the environment from a safe distance, defensively, without trying to stop the release8 hours, or demonstrated competency
Hazardous materials technicianApproaches the point of release to plug, patch or stop it24 hours at operations-level content, plus listed competencies
Hazardous materials specialistSupports technicians with specific knowledge of substances24 hours at technician-level content, plus competencies
On-scene incident commanderTakes control of the incident above the awareness level24 hours at operations-level content, plus listed competencies

Awareness: the most common, and most misunderstood

Awareness-level responders don't contain, absorb or stop anything. They recognize, isolate and notify. If your security guard is expected to throw absorbent on a leaking drum, they aren't awareness level anymore. Either train them to operations level, or change the plan.

Competency, not just hours

Each level comes with competencies the employer must be able to show. For example, an awareness responder must understand what hazardous substances are, recognize their presence, identify them if possible, understand their role in the plan and know how to notify. Certificates prove hours. Drills prove competency.

Every year

Under (q)(8), responders need annual refresher training of enough content and length to keep their competencies, or a yearly demonstration of competency. Keep a record of whichever method you use.
